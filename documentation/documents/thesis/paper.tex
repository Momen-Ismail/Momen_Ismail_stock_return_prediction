% Abstract

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This paper compares linear, regularized, dimension-reduction and tree-ensemble
methods for predicting next-month stock excess returns using a high-dimensional
monthly panel of firm characteristics, accounting information and aggregate
economic states. The empirical design uses chronological training, validation
and test samples, with annual expanding-window folds for hyperparameter
selection. Final results are reported after the selected specifications are
refitted on the complete development sample and evaluated on the untouched
2020--2025 test period.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.4em}

\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:}
stock return prediction, machine learning, high-dimensional predictors,
out-of-sample evaluation

% 1. Introduction

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Expected stock returns are central to asset pricing, portfolio allocation and
risk management. Predicting them is nevertheless difficult because realized
monthly returns contain substantial idiosyncratic variation and because
predictive relationships may change across firms and market regimes. A model
that explains historical observations well may therefore perform poorly when
applied to genuinely future data.

Machine-learning methods are potentially useful in this setting because they
can process large sets of correlated predictors, impose regularization and
represent nonlinearities and interactions. These properties are particularly
relevant when the available information includes firm characteristics,
accounting variables, market conditions and aggregate economic states.
However, greater model flexibility also increases the risk of overfitting.
A meaningful empirical comparison must therefore use chronological validation,
an untouched test sample and a transparent benchmark.

This paper addresses the following research question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can linear, regularized, dimension-reduction and tree-ensemble methods
improve next-month stock excess-return forecasts relative to a historical-mean
benchmark?}
\end{quote}

The empirical analysis compares six forecasting approaches under a common
information set and evaluation framework: the historical mean, a parsimonious
three-variable OLS model, Partial Least Squares, Elastic Net, Random Forest and
Gradient Boosting. The high-dimensional specifications use 484 predictors,
including stock characteristics, accounting information, market and VIX
variables, Welch--Goyal aggregate states, SIC2 industry controls and
characteristic--macro interactions.

The empirical design respects the chronological nature of financial
prediction. The initial training period runs from February 1990 through
December 2014. Hyperparameters are selected using annual expanding-window
validation folds within this period. Fixed and optimized specifications are
then compared using the January 2015--December 2019 validation sample. Once
the specifications are fixed, the training and validation observations are
combined into a development sample, and the models are refitted before being
evaluated once on the January 2020--December 2025 test period.

The study is related to the predictive asset-pricing literature that examines
whether large sets of firm characteristics can improve expected-return
forecasts. In particular, \textcite{gu2020empirical} show how machine-learning
methods can accommodate nonlinearities and interactions in high-dimensional
asset-pricing data. The analysis also follows the benchmark-oriented
perspective of \textcite{welch2008comprehensive}, who demonstrate that many
apparently sophisticated predictive models struggle to outperform simple
historical forecasts outside the estimation sample.

The contribution of the paper is not a new forecasting algorithm. Instead, it
provides a transparent and reproducible comparison of models with different
levels of complexity under a common timing structure. Particular attention is
given to leakage prevention, expanding-window tuning, comparison with the
historical-mean benchmark and interpretation of both positive and negative
results. This is important because a favorable model ranking does not
necessarily imply strong economic predictability or a profitable trading
strategy.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. \Cref{sec:data} describes
the data sources, target construction, predictor set and chronological sample
design. \Cref{sec:methodology} presents the forecasting models, tuning
procedure and evaluation criteria. \Cref{sec:results} reports validation and
final test results together with model-interpretation evidence. The subsequent
section discusses limitations and reproducibility, and the final section
concludes.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Sources and observation unit}

The observation unit is a stock-month. The stock universe is based on a locked
S\&P 500-style ticker list that includes both current and historical securities
available in the project inputs. The universe is held fixed throughout the
production pipeline so that all data-construction and modeling scripts use the
same set of ticker identifiers.

Daily stock-price and volume information is obtained from Yahoo Finance. The
daily S\&P 500 index and VIX series are used to construct aggregate market and
volatility variables. Monthly Fama--French data provide the one-month
risk-free rate used to calculate excess returns. Annual Compustat data provide
accounting characteristics and industry classifications. Aggregate predictive
states are obtained from the Welch--Goyal data set.

Daily price histories begin in 1987 so that long-window characteristics, such
as 12-month beta and longer-horizon momentum, can be constructed before the
start of the modeling sample. After data construction and cleaning, the final
monthly panel covers February 1990 through December 2025.

\subsection{Target construction}

For stock \(i\) in month \(t\), the forecasting target is the stock's excess
return in the immediately following calendar month:

\begin{equation}
 y_{i,t+1}
 =
 r_{i,t+1}-RF_{t+1},
 \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}

where \(r_{i,t+1}\) is the stock return in month \(t+1\) and \(RF_{t+1}\) is
the corresponding one-month risk-free rate.

The target is constructed only when the next observation for the same ticker
belongs to the immediately following calendar month. Observations followed by
a gap in the ticker's monthly history are therefore not treated as valid
one-month-ahead targets. This prevents a return measured over several calendar
months from being incorrectly interpreted as a one-month return.

The target remains in its original return units. It is not cross-sectionally
rank-normalized and is not winsorized in the common model sample. January 2026
price information may be used only to realize the target associated with a
December 2025 predictor row. The final set of predictor observations therefore
ends in December 2025.

\subsection{Information timing and leakage controls}

All predictors attached to a row dated month \(t\) are constructed using
information available by the end of that month. Stock characteristics are
calculated from current and historical price information through month \(t\).
They do not use the return realized in month \(t+1\).

Annual Compustat observations require special treatment because Compustat
\texttt{datadate} records the fiscal-year-end date rather than the date on
which the accounting information became publicly available. Each annual report
is therefore treated as available only six months after its fiscal-year end.
For every ticker-month, a backward as-of merge assigns the most recent
Compustat report whose assumed availability date is not later than the stock
month. Accounting observations that have become excessively stale are not
carried forward indefinitely.

Welch--Goyal aggregate states are lagged by one month before entering the
predictor set. Consequently, a predictor row dated month \(t\) uses the
aggregate state recorded for month \(t-1\). This conservative timing rule
reduces the possibility that information published only after the end of month
\(t\) enters the forecast.

The combination of consecutive-month target construction, lagged accounting
information and lagged aggregate states ensures that future information is not
used in the predictors.

\subsection{Missing values and cross-sectional transformation}

Missing predictor observations are replaced using the cross-sectional median
among firms observed in the same month. This procedure uses no information
from future months. When an entire predictor is unavailable for all firms in a
particular month, its remaining missing values are set to zero.

Continuous predictors are subsequently transformed into within-month
cross-sectional ranks on the interval \([-1,1]\):

\begin{equation}
 x^{\mathrm{rank}}_{i,t}
 =
 2
 \frac{
   \operatorname{rank}(x_{i,t})-1
 }{
   N_t-1
 }
 -1,
 \label{eq:rank-transform}
\end{equation}

where \(N_t\) is the number of firms observed in month \(t\). The transformation
preserves the cross-sectional ordering of firms while reducing sensitivity to
units, skewness and extreme predictor values.

Binary variables and SIC2 industry indicators remain in their natural
indicator form rather than being rank-normalized. The return target also
remains untransformed.

\subsection{Predictor set and final panel}

The final model data contain 484 predictors. These can be divided into 124
base predictors and 360 interaction variables.

The 124 base predictors consist of:

\begin{itemize}
  \item 45 continuous stock and accounting characteristics;
  \item 3 binary stock characteristics;
  \item 4 aggregate market and VIX variables;
  \item 8 lagged Welch--Goyal state variables;
  \item 64 SIC2 industry indicators.
\end{itemize}

The continuous stock and accounting characteristics capture economically
important dimensions including:

\begin{itemize}
  \item size and valuation;
  \item profitability and cash flow;
  \item investment and asset growth;
  \item leverage and liquidity;
  \item short- and long-horizon momentum;
  \item return reversals and price trends;
  \item volatility, downside risk, beta and idiosyncratic volatility;
  \item trading activity and market liquidity.
\end{itemize}

To allow the relationship between firm characteristics and future returns to
vary with aggregate economic conditions, each of the 45 continuous
characteristics is interacted with each of the eight Welch--Goyal state
variables:

\begin{equation}
 z_{i,t}^{(j,k)}
 =
 x_{i,t}^{(j)}
 \times
 w_{t}^{(k)},
 \label{eq:interactions}
\end{equation}

where \(x_{i,t}^{(j)}\) denotes firm characteristic \(j\) and
\(w_t^{(k)}\) denotes aggregate state \(k\). This produces

\[
45 \times 8 = 360
\]

characteristic--state interactions.

The final model-ready panel contains 211,059 stock-month observations for 656
tickers. It contains no missing predictor values, infinite values or duplicate
ticker-month observations. The large number of predictors and the substantial
correlation among characteristics motivate the use of regularization,
dimension reduction and nonlinear ensemble methods.

\subsection{Chronological sample design}

The final panel is divided chronologically into the following samples:

\begin{itemize}
  \item training sample: February 1990--December 2014;
  \item validation sample: January 2015--December 2019;
  \item development sample: February 1990--December 2019;
  \item test sample: January 2020--December 2025.
\end{itemize}

Hyperparameters are selected using annual expanding-window folds within the
training sample. The first tuning fold estimates the model using observations
through December 2004 and evaluates it during 2005. The estimation window is
then expanded by one year at a time until the final fold, which estimates the
model through December 2013 and evaluates it during 2014.

Fixed and tuned specifications are compared using the separate 2015--2019
validation sample. After the final specifications have been selected, the
training and validation observations are combined into the development sample.
Each model is then refitted on the complete development period and evaluated
once on the 2020--2025 test sample.

Random cross-validation is not used because it would mix earlier and later
market observations and allow future conditions to influence model selection.
The expanding-window design instead approximates the information sequence
faced by an investor who repeatedly estimates a model using only data available
at the forecast date.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Forecasting framework}

For stock \(i\) in month \(t\), the general forecasting problem is

\begin{equation}
 y_{i,t+1}
 =
 f(\mathbf{x}_{i,t})
 +
 \varepsilon_{i,t+1},
 \label{eq:forecast}
\end{equation}

where \(y_{i,t+1}\) is the next-month stock excess return,
\(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}\) is the vector of predictors observed by the end of month
\(t\), and the function \(f(\cdot)\) differs across forecasting models.

All models are evaluated using the same target, predictor information and
chronological sample design. This common framework ensures that performance
differences reflect the forecasting methods rather than differences in the
underlying data.

\subsection{Forecasting models}

The analysis compares six models with different levels of complexity.

\textbf{Historical Mean.}
The benchmark predicts the average target observed in the permitted estimation
sample:

\begin{equation}
 \widehat{y}^{\mathrm{HM}}_{i,t+1}
 =
 \frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{est}}}
 \sum_{(j,s)\in\mathcal{E}}
 y_{j,s+1},
 \label{eq:historical-mean}
\end{equation}

where \(\mathcal{E}\) denotes the observations available in the relevant
estimation period. The prediction is identical for all stocks in a forecast
sample. During final testing, the benchmark is estimated using the complete
February 1990--December 2019 development sample.

\textbf{OLS-3.}
The parsimonious OLS specification uses three economically motivated
predictors: firm size, book-to-market and 12-month momentum. The model is

\begin{equation}
 y_{i,t+1}
 =
 \beta_0
 +
 \beta_1 \mathrm{Size}_{i,t}
 +
 \beta_2 \mathrm{BM}_{i,t}
 +
 \beta_3 \mathrm{Mom12}_{i,t}
 +
 \varepsilon_{i,t+1}.
 \label{eq:ols3}
\end{equation}

OLS-3 provides an interpretable reference between the constant benchmark and
the high-dimensional models. Its predictors are standardized before
estimation, although this scaling does not change the fitted values of an
unregularized linear regression.

\textbf{Partial Least Squares.}
Partial Least Squares (PLS) reduces the 484-dimensional predictor space to a
small number of supervised latent components. In contrast to unsupervised
dimension-reduction methods, PLS constructs components that are related to both
the predictors and the forecasting target. It is therefore well suited to a
setting with many strongly correlated characteristics
\parencite{wold1984collinearity}.

The predictors are standardized before PLS estimation. The number of
components is selected using expanding-window validation.

\textbf{Elastic Net.}
Elastic Net estimates a high-dimensional linear model while combining
\(L_1\) and \(L_2\) regularization:

\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
 =
 \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
 \left[
 \frac{1}{2n}
 \left\|
 \mathbf{y}
 -
 \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}
 \right\|_2^2
 +
 \alpha
 \left(
 \rho
 \left\|
 \boldsymbol{\beta}
 \right\|_1
 +
 \frac{1-\rho}{2}
 \left\|
 \boldsymbol{\beta}
 \right\|_2^2
 \right)
 \right].
 \label{eq:elastic-net}
\end{equation}

The parameter \(\alpha\) controls the overall strength of regularization, while
\(\rho\) controls the relative weight assigned to the \(L_1\) penalty. The
\(L_1\) component permits coefficient shrinkage to exactly zero, while the
\(L_2\) component stabilizes estimates when predictors are strongly correlated
\parencite{tibshirani1996regression,zou2005regularization}. All predictors are
standardized before estimation.

\textbf{Random Forest.}
Random Forest estimates many regression trees using bootstrap samples and
averages their predictions \parencite{breiman2001random}. At each split, only a
random subset of predictors is considered. This randomization reduces
correlation among the trees and lowers the variance associated with an
individual regression tree.

The specification uses the square root of the number of predictors as the
candidate set at each split, a minimum terminal-leaf size of 20 observations,
bootstrap sampling and out-of-bag evaluation. Out-of-bag performance is used
only as a development-sample diagnostic and is not treated as final
out-of-sample evidence.

\textbf{Gradient Boosting.}
Gradient Boosting constructs an additive prediction function sequentially:

\begin{equation}
 \widehat{f}_{M}(\mathbf{x})
 =
 \widehat{f}_{M-1}(\mathbf{x})
 +
 \eta h_M(\mathbf{x}),
 \label{eq:gradient-boosting}
\end{equation}

where \(h_M(\mathbf{x})\) is a shallow regression tree fitted at boosting
iteration \(M\), and \(\eta\) is the learning rate. Each new tree attempts to
correct the errors remaining from the previous ensemble.

A small learning rate and shallow trees restrict the contribution of each
additional learner. The number of trees, learning rate and maximum tree depth
are examined during expanding-window tuning.

\subsection{Hyperparameter tuning}

Hyperparameters are selected using ten annual expanding-window folds within the
February 1990--December 2014 training sample.

The first fold estimates each model using observations through December 2004
and evaluates it on 2005. The estimation window is then expanded by one year at
a time. The final fold estimates the model using observations through December
2013 and evaluates it on 2014.

For a hyperparameter combination \(\lambda\), the tuning criterion is the
average monthly MSE across the ten folds:

\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{CV\text{-}MSE}(\lambda)
 =
 \frac{1}{K}
 \sum_{k=1}^{K}
 \operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{month},k}(\lambda),
 \qquad K=10.
 \label{eq:cv-mse}
\end{equation}

The selected tuning results are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item PLS: 2 components;
  \item Elastic Net: \(\alpha=0.015\) and \(\rho=0.85\);
  \item Random Forest: 300 trees;
  \item Gradient Boosting: 300 trees, learning rate \(0.01\), and maximum
        depth 2.
\end{itemize}

These tuning results identify the best hyperparameters within the expanding
training folds. They do not automatically determine the final specification,
because the tuned models are subsequently compared with their fixed
counterparts using a separate validation sample.

\subsection{Validation-based specification selection}

After tuning, fixed and optimized specifications are evaluated on the
January 2015--December 2019 validation period.

The optimized PLS model produced a lower validation MSE than the fixed
20-component PLS specification. The optimized Elastic Net also improved on its
fixed counterpart, although the selected penalty shrank all 484 coefficients
to zero and therefore produced a forecast equivalent to the historical mean.

For both tree ensembles, the fixed 100-tree specifications performed better on
the separate validation sample than the tuned 300-tree versions. The final
Random Forest therefore uses:

\begin{itemize}
  \item 100 trees;
  \item square-root feature sampling;
  \item minimum leaf size of 20;
  \item bootstrap sampling.
\end{itemize}

The final Gradient Boosting specification uses:

\begin{itemize}
  \item 100 trees;
  \item learning rate \(0.01\);
  \item maximum tree depth 2.
\end{itemize}

This two-stage procedure distinguishes between hyperparameter performance
inside the expanding training folds and performance in the later validation
period. The final specifications are selected before examining the
2020--2025 test results.

\subsection{Final estimation and testing}

After the final specifications are selected, the training and validation
observations are combined into the February 1990--December 2019 development
sample. Every model is re-estimated using this complete development sample and
then evaluated once on the January 2020--December 2025 test sample.

The test sample contains 44,612 stock-month observations over 72 months. No
hyperparameters, predictors or model specifications are changed after examining
the test results.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

The primary evaluation criterion is monthly mean squared error:

\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{month}}
 =
 \frac{1}{T}
 \sum_{t=1}^{T}
 \left[
 \frac{1}{N_t}
 \sum_{i=1}^{N_t}
 \left(
 y_{i,t+1}
 -
 \widehat{y}_{i,t+1}
 \right)^2
 \right].
 \label{eq:monthly-mse}
\end{equation}

Forecast errors are first averaged across firms within each month and then
across months. Consequently, every month receives equal weight, regardless of
the number of stocks observed in that month.

The corresponding monthly root mean squared error is

\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{RMSE}_{\mathrm{month}}
 =
 \sqrt{
 \operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{month}}
 }.
 \label{eq:monthly-rmse}
\end{equation}

The monthly out-of-sample \(R^2\) compares a model's monthly MSE with that of
the historical-mean benchmark:

\begin{equation}
 R^2_{\mathrm{OOS,month}}
 =
 1
 -
 \frac{
   \operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{month,model}}
 }{
   \operatorname{MSE}_{\mathrm{month,HM}}
 }.
 \label{eq:monthly-oos-r2}
\end{equation}

A positive value means that the model reduces average monthly squared forecast
error relative to the historical mean. A negative value means that it performs
worse than the benchmark.

For completeness, the analysis also reports pooled MSE, RMSE and MAE, where all
stock-month observations receive equal weight. Pooled out-of-sample \(R^2\) is
defined as

\begin{equation}
 R^2_{\mathrm{OOS,pooled}}
 =
 1
 -
 \frac{
   \sum_{i,t}
   \left(
   y_{i,t+1}
   -
   \widehat{y}_{i,t+1}
   \right)^2
 }{
   \sum_{i,t}
   \left(
   y_{i,t+1}
   -
   \widehat{y}^{\mathrm{HM}}_{i,t+1}
   \right)^2
 }.
 \label{eq:pooled-oos-r2}
\end{equation}

The report additionally presents the correlation between predictions and
realized excess returns. This measure captures whether cross-sectional
variation in forecasts is aligned with realized outcomes, but it does not
measure forecast calibration or investment profitability. Correlation is
undefined for the historical mean and the final Elastic Net model because
their predictions are constant or effectively constant.

error by approximately \(0.18\%\) relative to the historical mean. The result
is positive, but economically small.

Random Forest ranks second with an out-of-sample \(R^2\) of approximately
\(0.058\%\). OLS-3 ranks third with approximately \(0.049\%\). The difference
between these two models is minimal, indicating that the large increase in
model complexity delivered by Random Forest produces only a small additional
reduction in squared error relative to the parsimonious OLS specification.

Elastic Net and the historical mean produce almost identical results. The
optimized Elastic Net contains no coefficients above the specified tolerance
and therefore behaves as a constant predictor. This is a meaningful negative
finding: after chronological validation, the regularized model found no
sufficiently stable incremental signal among the 499 predictors.

The Decision Tree performs considerably worse than all other models. Its
out-of-sample \(R^2\) is approximately \(-13.3\%\), and its prediction-target
correlation is negative. The individual tree appears to fit unstable splits
that do not generalize to the test sample.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[
    width=0.86\textwidth,
    trim=5 5 5 22,
    clip
  ]{\FigurePath/test_oos_r2_by_model.png}

  \caption{Preliminary test-period out-of-sample \(R^2\) by model}
  \label{fig:test-oos-r2}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:test-oos-r2} emphasizes both the ranking and the limited absolute
predictive power. PLS, Random Forest and OLS-3 outperform the benchmark, but all
positive values remain close to zero. The Decision Tree is qualitatively
different because its additional flexibility substantially increases forecast
error.

The MAE ranking differs slightly from the MSE ranking. The historical mean and
Elastic Net have the lowest MAE, while PLS has the lowest MSE. This suggests
that PLS reduces some relatively large forecast errors, which receive more
weight under squared loss, without reducing the typical absolute error.

\subsection{Financial interpretation}

The small positive out-of-sample \(R^2\) values represent small reductions in
forecast error relative to the historical-mean benchmark. They provide evidence
of weak statistical predictability in the current project version, but they do
not establish profitable trading. In particular, MSE does not account for
portfolio turnover, transaction costs, liquidity, short-selling constraints or
risk adjustment. Nor does it show that the cross-sectional ordering of forecasts
can be converted into stable implementable positions. The present evidence
should therefore be interpreted as a preliminary comparison of forecasting
methods, not as a ready-to-use investment strategy.

\subsection{Performance across test years}

The preliminary pooled ranking does not imply stable performance in every year. PLS and
Random Forest achieve positive yearly out-of-sample \(R^2\) in half of the six
test years. OLS-3 does so in one third of the years. The Decision Tree beats the
benchmark in only one year.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[
    width=0.90\textwidth,

  ]{\FigurePath/yearly_oos_r2_by_model.png}

  \caption{Out-of-sample \(R^2\) by model and test year}
  \label{fig:yearly-oos-r2}
\end{figure}

The yearly results demonstrate that even the best overall model does not
consistently outperform the historical mean. This instability is important
because the test period contains only six years and includes unusually
different market environments. The overall advantage of PLS should therefore
be interpreted as an average test-period result rather than evidence of stable
performance in every regime.

\subsection{Forecast dispersion}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[
    width=0.90\textwidth,
    trim=5 5 5 22,
    clip
  ]{\FigurePath/prediction_distribution_by_model.png}

  \caption{Distribution of preliminary test-period predictions}
  \label{fig:prediction-distribution}
\end{figure}

The prediction distributions help explain the model results.
Elastic Net is absent as a meaningful distribution because it produces
constant or near-constant forecasts. PLS and OLS generate relatively
concentrated predictions, while the tree-based models produce more dispersed
forecasts. Dispersion alone does not indicate predictive quality. The Decision
Tree produces variable forecasts but performs poorly because those forecasts
are systematically misaligned with realized returns.

\subsection{Feature importance}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[
    width=0.88\textwidth,
    trim=5 5 5 5,
    clip
  ]{\FigurePath/top_pls_coefficients.png}

  \caption{Largest PLS coefficients by absolute magnitude}
  \label{fig:pls-coefficients}
\end{figure}

PLS coefficients measure conditional predictive associations after
preprocessing and scaling. Their magnitude should not be interpreted as a
causal effect or as an estimated risk premium. Because PLS combines information
through latent components, the coefficients reflect the joint high-dimensional
prediction problem rather than isolated univariate relationships.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[
    width=0.88\textwidth,
    trim=5 5 5 5,
    clip
  ]{\FigurePath/top_random_forest_features.png}

  \caption{Top Random Forest predictors based on impurity importance}
  \label{fig:rf-importance}
\end{figure}

Random Forest importance records how strongly variables reduce squared error
inside the fitted trees. It is useful for describing where the model obtains
its predictive variation, but it has important limitations. Correlated
variables can divide importance among themselves, and impurity importance can
favor predictors with more available split points. The results should therefore
be interpreted at the level of broad predictor groups rather than as a precise
ranking of causal variables.

% 5. Critical reflection

\section{Critical Reflection}
\label{sec:critical-reflection}

\subsection{Predictive gains are small}

The main limitation is the weak absolute predictive performance. Although PLS
ranks first, its out-of-sample \(R^2\) is only approximately \(0.18\%\). Random
Forest and OLS-3 improve on the benchmark by even less. Most variation in
monthly stock returns therefore remains unexplained.

A ranking can still be statistically informative, but it should not be confused
with strong forecasting ability. The results do not establish that the small
differences between the leading models would remain stable in another period.

\subsection{Flexible models do not automatically perform better}

The results provide two important failures. First, the individual Decision Tree
performs much worse than the historical mean. Its threshold splits are
apparently too unstable for noisy monthly return data. Random Forest improves
substantially by averaging across trees, illustrating the benefit of variance
reduction.

Second, Elastic Net selects no nonzero coefficients. This does not imply a
software error. Given the selected tuning parameters and chronological
validation procedure, the model preferred complete shrinkage over unstable
predictor effects. The result suggests that individual linear signals are weak
relative to the noise in the target.

\subsection{Implementation issues identified during the project audit}

The project audit identified six implementation and reproducibility problems
that prevent the current outputs from being described as final results:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The ticker-level target lead contains 26 nonconsecutive transitions in
        which the next available observation is not the immediately following
        calendar month.
  \item Welch--Goyal macro variables are merged in the same month rather than
        with the intended explicit one-month availability lag.
  \item The documentation and implementation are inconsistent about the tuning
        periods and the model-selection rule.
  \item The top-level \texttt{run\_pipeline.sh} became stale after the model
        code was reorganized and references stages that no longer match the
        active workflow.
  \item The output structure contains excessive duplicate and legacy artifacts,
        which makes it harder to identify the authoritative results.
  \item Software dependencies are not pinned to exact versions, limiting exact
        reproducibility across environments.
\end{itemize}

These issues do not make the current tables useless: they remain informative
about model behavior and expose several robust negative findings. They do mean,
however, that the numerical rankings must be treated as preliminary. Before the
complete rebuild, the target continuity rule and macro lag will be corrected,
the tuning design and documentation will be reconciled, the pipeline entry
point will be repaired, dependencies will be pinned, and authoritative outputs
will be separated from legacy artifacts. The existing output folder will then
be deleted and regenerated from the permanent inputs in one clean chronological
run.

\subsection{Data limitations}

The stock universe is not a fully point-in-time CRSP constituent universe.
Ticker-based matching may be affected by symbol changes, mergers and incomplete
historical membership records. Survivorship and universe-selection bias cannot
be ruled out completely.

Yahoo Finance does not provide the security master, delisting returns and
microstructure information available in CRSP. Adjusted price histories may also
be revised. Compustat fundamentals are lagged by six months, but exact public
filing dates are not used. The resulting timing design is conservative, though
not perfectly point in time.

The use of annual Compustat information also means that accounting variables
change less frequently than market variables. Some predictors are proxies for
variables used in the published asset-pricing literature and may not reproduce
their original definitions exactly.

\subsection{Validation and specification risk}

Annual expanding-window validation respects chronology and reduces direct
leakage. Nevertheless, the project contains many researcher choices:
predictor construction, interaction design, preprocessing, tuning grids and
sample dates. These decisions may adapt indirectly to known characteristics of
the data.

An untouched holdout period remains credible only if no model changes are
made after inspecting its performance. The current 2020--2025 outputs have
already been examined and are therefore reported only as preliminary evidence.
The corrected workflow must be specified before the clean rebuild, and any
subsequent specification search would require a genuinely new holdout period.

\subsection{No economic trading evaluation}

The project evaluates prediction error but does not construct portfolios.
Consequently, it does not measure turnover, transaction costs, market impact,
short-sale constraints or risk-adjusted investment returns. A small reduction
in MSE cannot automatically be interpreted as an economically profitable
strategy.

A useful extension would specify a portfolio rule before opening a new test
sample. The evaluation could then report gross and net returns, volatility,
Sharpe ratios, turnover and maximum drawdown. That analysis would have to be
kept separate from the current test period to avoid using the same observations
for model assessment and strategy design.

\subsection{What I would do differently}

With improved data access and additional time, the study could be strengthened
in four ways:

\begin{itemize}
  \item use a point-in-time CRSP/Compustat universe with permanent identifiers
        and delisting returns;
  \item use exact filing dates for accounting information;
  \item retain a longer untouched test period or evaluate several historical
        forecast origins;
  \item conduct a pre-specified portfolio evaluation with realistic trading
        costs.
\end{itemize}

Interpretability could also be improved by examining feature groups, repeated
time-block importance and stability across different expanding windows rather
than relying on a single definitive importance ranking.

% ============================================================
% 6. Conclusion
% ============================================================

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This preliminary report examines whether machine-learning methods improve next-month stock
excess-return prediction relative to a historical-mean benchmark. The models
are selected chronologically and evaluated on a held-out 2020--2025 test
period in the current implementation.

PLS performs best according to monthly mean squared error and produces an
out-of-sample \(R^2\) of approximately \(0.18\%\). Random Forest and OLS-3 also
slightly outperform the benchmark. Elastic Net selects no predictors and
effectively collapses to the historical mean. The Decision Tree performs
substantially worse, showing that flexibility without sufficient variance
control can be harmful.

The research question can therefore be answered cautiously. Some statistical
and machine-learning methods improve on the historical mean, but the gains are
small and unstable across years. The results do not demonstrate strong return
predictability or a profitable investment strategy. Their principal lesson is
that, in noisy financial prediction problems, disciplined chronology, simple
benchmarks and honest reporting of failures are at least as important as model
complexity. Because the audit identified target-continuity, macro-timing and
workflow inconsistencies, these results are not the final project evidence.
Those issues will be corrected before the output folder is deleted and the
project is rebuilt cleanly from its permanent inputs.

\section{Code and Reproducibility Overview}
\label{app:code-overview}

The repository implements the empirical workflow as a sequence of auditable
stages rather than embedding source code in this report:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.2em,topsep=0.25em]
  \item acquisition of permanent external inputs and cleaning of daily stock
        histories;
  \item construction of monthly stock features and the next-month target;
  \item merging of lagged fundamentals and aggregate macroeconomic variables;
  \item final missing-value treatment and within-month rank normalization;
  \item chronological hyperparameter tuning with expanding estimation windows;
  \item preliminary test-period refitting and evaluation;
  \item interpretation, figure generation and report production.
\end{enumerate}

The complete Python and shell code is provided in the accompanying repository.
This document reads its principal tables and figures from the existing
interpretation outputs and does not retrain models or regenerate predictions
during compilation. Before the final clean rebuild, the audited timing,
workflow and dependency issues described in
\cref{sec:critical-reflection} will be corrected.
